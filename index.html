ABOUT THE PROJECT

The data, the design, and our process

Dataset and focus

The supplied weather dataset contains 416,937 daily records across 1,591 station locations, dated January 1–September 21, 2017. Our view focuses on 12 airport stations across the eastern, central, and western United States to keep comparisons readable while showing different weather patterns. We use daily maximum temperature (TMAX) and precipitation (PRCP) to define a comfortable day; daily minimum temperature (TMIN) provides context in tooltips and a consistency check. Comfort here reflects the user's two preferences, rather than a measure that also accounts for humidity, wind, or conditions throughout the day.

Design rationale and alternatives

The heatmap aligns dates horizontally and stations vertically, making seasonal patterns and runs of comfortable days easy to compare. A fixed blue-to-warm color scale represents daily highs consistently across stations and selections. Dark outlines mark days meeting both preferences without replacing the temperature colors. Dates outside the selected window fade to preserve context; gray cells distinguish missing or excluded measurements.

Sorted horizontal bars use length on a shared 0–100% scale to compare the share of valid days that are comfortable. The selected station's line chart uses position for a more precise view of daily highs, with a shaded temperature band and green dots for days also meeting the rain limit. Compared with a map, this layout gives more space to changes over time; compared with 12 overlapping lines, it reduces clutter. Monthly averages would be simpler but would hide individual days and consecutive streaks. These tradeoffs explain the final layout.

Implemented interactions

  • Preference sliders: change minimum and maximum daily highs and the rain limit, updating the linked charts and summaries immediately.
  • Date selection: drag or resize the date brush for quick exploration, or enter exact start and end dates. “Show all dates” restores the full period.
  • Region filtering: compare all stations or a smaller East, Central, or West group.
  • Station selection: click a heatmap cell, station label, or ranking bar to update the detail chart. Station labels and ranking bars also support Enter and Space.
  • Details on hover: inspect daily readings in the heatmap and line chart, or counts and missing days in the ranking. Tooltips keep exact values available without crowding the charts.
  • Reset: restore the initial preferences, dates, region, and station.

Data resources and methods

Our data source is weather.csv, provided with the course assignment. Temperatures are interpreted as degrees Fahrenheit and precipitation as inches; the original documentation is still needed to confirm these units. Blank or nonnumeric values, temperatures outside −80 to 140°F, negative precipitation, and highs below the recorded low are excluded as applicable. Comfortable-day percentages require both a valid high and precipitation reading; missing days break streaks. Airport observations and a partial year do not establish a citywide or long-term climate ranking.

The charts use D3.js v7, including its scales and date-brushing API.

Jack also wrote the project write-ups, covering the dataset, design rationale, interactions, references, and development process.

Shared work and effort. Both team members contributed to styling the webpage and bringing their components together into a consistent interface. We spent approximately 8–10 people-hours combined over the past week. A substantial portion of that time went into deciding what to visualize and how to help viewers develop useful insights. That planning shaped the scope of the project and the questions the finished application supports.
